    Units

    Unit 1 — Semiconductor ecosystem and scaling fundamentals · 6 L + 6 P

    Design, fabrication, packaging and product chain; self-reliance and the Indian Semiconductor Mission. Advantages of microfabrication and scaling; Moore's Law, Dennard scaling, the interconnect bottleneck. FEOL and BEOL. Cleanroom classes and contamination control. Mask layout for NMOS, PMOS and the inverter; DRC concepts; relating layout layers to the physical cross-section.

    Unit 2 — Lithography and patterning · 6 L + 6 P

    Optical lithography; positive and negative photoresist chemistry; spin coat, soft bake, exposure, development. Resolution limit and the Rayleigh criterion. Exposure source evolution: i-line → KrF → ArF → EUV. Resolution enhancement: OPC, PSM, SRAF. Immersion lithography. Multiple patterning — LELE, SADP and SAQP process flows, overlay budget, sub-7 nm application. Cost and complexity trade-offs; design-rule implications.

    Unit 3 — Advanced CMOS FinFET process: FEOL modules · 6 L + 6 P

    Isolation: LOCOS versus STI, stress effects, integration advantages. Device architecture: planar MOSFET to FinFET; fin formation, aspect ratio, SiGe and strain engineering. Gate module: SiO₂ versus high-k (HfO₂), polysilicon versus metal gate, gate-first versus gate-last, replacement metal gate flow, work-function metal selection. Well and junction module: implantation dose and energy, LDD and halo implants, RTA activation. Spacer engineering and the contact etch stop layer.

    Unit 4 — Advanced CMOS FinFET process: contact and silicide modules · 6 L + 6 P

    Contact resistance in a simple metal–silicon contact; metal silicides and self-aligned silicidation; evolution from Ti to Co to Ni silicide — thermal stability, junction leakage, FinFET integration. Contact plug formation: tungsten plug, TiN and TaN barriers. Contact scaling at advanced nodes. Thermal budget constraints.

    Unit 5 — Interconnect and dielectric modules (BEOL) · 6 L + 6 P

    Multilevel interconnect; RC delay and electromigration; aluminium to copper. Damascene and dual-Damascene flows; barrier and seed layers, copper electroplating, CMP. Interlayer dielectric scaling: SiO₂ → SiOF → SiOC → porous SiOC; air-gap dielectrics. BEOL reliability — electromigration, stress voiding, TDDB. Technology roadmap from 180 nm to 7 nm; full FEOL-to-BEOL integration review; yield and manufacturability.

    Sample micro-lesson 3.3 — Why the gate had to change

    Unit 3 · CO3 and CO4 · 11 minutes of video, 25 minutes of practice, 90 minutes of laboratory.

    What you will be able to do

    1. Explain why a polysilicon gate on silicon dioxide stopped scaling below roughly the 65 nm node.
    2. Calculate equivalent oxide thickness for a high-k dielectric and justify why the physically thicker film leaks less at the same electrical thickness.
    3. Order the eight operations of a replacement metal gate flow and say what would fail if the sequence were gate-first instead.

    The problem, stated as an engineer would state it

    Gate capacitance per unit area is C = ε₀k/t. Scaling the transistor means raising that capacitance, which means thinning the dielectric. By the 90 nm node, gate oxide had reached about 1.2 nm — roughly five atomic layers of SiO₂. At that thickness electrons tunnel directly through the dielectric, and gate leakage current begins to dominate the chip's standby power budget. You cannot thin the film further. The scaling path is blocked by quantum mechanics, not by lithography.

    Three separate failures arrive at once, and it is worth naming them separately because students routinely collapse them into one:

    • Direct tunnelling through the gate dielectric. Leakage rises roughly exponentially as physical thickness falls.
    • Polysilicon depletion. A depletion region forms at the poly–oxide interface under inversion bias, adding a capacitance in series with the oxide. This adds roughly 0.3–0.4 nm to the effective electrical thickness — a large penalty when the oxide itself is 1.2 nm.
    • Fermi-level pinning. Put polysilicon directly on a hafnium-based dielectric and the gate's effective work function is pinned by interface states, so threshold voltage can no longer be set by doping the gate.

    The fix addresses all three. Replace SiO₂ with a higher-permittivity dielectric, so the film can be made physically thicker while keeping the same electrical thickness. Replace polysilicon with a metal, which has no depletion region and whose work function can be chosen by selecting the metal.

    Why the sequence has to be gate-last

    A hafnium oxide and metal gate stack does not tolerate the source–drain activation anneal, which runs above 1000 °C. Deposit the real gate first and that anneal degrades the dielectric interface and shifts the work function of the metal, so threshold voltage drifts in ways that cannot be corrected downstream.

    The replacement metal gate flow sidesteps this by building a sacrificial polysilicon gate, using it to self-align the spacers and the source–drain regions, taking the high-temperature steps, and only then removing the dummy gate and filling the empty trench with the real stack. The high-k and the work-function metal never see more than a few hundred degrees. Step through the eight operations in the panel at the top of this page and watch where the dummy gate disappears.

    Narration, 2:30 – 4:05

    ANIMATION — two dielectric stacks side by side, identical capacitance

    Here are two capacitors. Electrically, they are the same: same area, same capacitance per unit area, so the transistor sees the same gate control from either. The one on the left is silicon dioxide, 1.2 nanometres thick. The one on the right is hafnium oxide, and it is 4.4 nanometres thick — nearly four times thicker.

    ANIMATION — electron wavefunctions decay across each barrier

    Now watch what an electron sees. Tunnelling probability falls off exponentially with the physical distance it has to cross. Not the electrical thickness — the physical one. On the left the barrier is five atoms wide and the electron gets through. On the right it has nearly four times as far to go, and the leakage current drops by orders of magnitude.

    ANNOTATION — EOT = t × 3.9/k written over the right-hand stack

    That is the whole trick, and it is worth saying plainly: the dielectric constant buys you physical thickness at constant electrical thickness. We call that electrical thickness the equivalent oxide thickness — the thickness of silicon dioxide that would have given you the same capacitance. Hafnium oxide has a dielectric constant around twenty-two against silicon dioxide's three point nine, so you get roughly five and a half times the physical thickness for free.

    CUT TO — poly gate, depletion region appearing

    So the dielectric problem is solved. Unfortunately, the moment you put hafnium oxide under a polysilicon gate, you create two new problems. Let us take them one at a time.

    2. Find the physical thickness of HfO₂ at k = 22 that gives EOT = 1.0 nm. Compare it with the SiO₂ film it replaces.

    Misconception check

    Delivered as a two-minute interstitial. Each item is a claim students routinely make in viva; the task is to say what is wrong with it, not to pick true or false.

    1. "High-k materials are used because they leak less than silicon dioxide." — The material is not intrinsically less leaky. It leaks less in the application because the same capacitance is obtained from a physically thicker film. Change the comparison to equal physical thickness and the argument disappears.
    2. "Equivalent oxide thickness is the real thickness of the gate stack." — It is an electrical equivalence, not a measurement. A cross-section will show a physically thicker film, plus an interfacial SiO₂ layer of a few ångström that adds to EOT and that no deposition recipe fully removes.
    3. "Gate-last is used because it is simpler." — It is markedly more complex: an extra deposition, an extra patterning, a CMP, a selective removal and a metal fill. It is used because it keeps the gate stack out of the high-temperature activation anneal. Complexity was accepted in exchange for thermal budget.

    Laboratory brief — Assignment 6

    Replacement metal gate: complete simulation with metal fill and CMP · SEMulator3D® · 2 hours supervised, 3 hours independent · CO5.

    Task

    Starting from the supplied dummy-gate process file, complete the replacement metal gate module. Deposit the contact etch stop layer and interlayer dielectric, planarize to expose the dummy gate, remove the polysilicon selectively, deposit the high-k dielectric and work-function metal conformally, fill with tungsten, and planarize. Extract a cross-section through the gate and report four measurements.

    Deliverables

    1. The completed process file and built model.
    2. Cross-section image with physical gate length, high-k thickness, work-function metal thickness and remaining tungsten fill height measured and annotated.
    3. One paragraph explaining what changed in the model at the poly-removal step, and what you would expect to see if the selective etch had a 10% loss of the spacer nitride.
    4. A single-page comparison against your gate-first build from Assignment 4.

    If your build fails

    A metrology step that returns NOVAL almost always means the measurement mask does not cover the feature you are measuring, not that the model is wrong. Check the manager log for the step that failed, open the metrology definition, confirm the measurement type against the help entry, then check the mask bounds in the layout editor. Rebuild from the failing step rather than rebuilding all. Bring the log and a screenshot of the metrology definition to the clinic session if it is still unresolved.

    Innovative pedagogy for this course

    Eight strategies, each chosen because a virtual fabrication platform makes it possible in a classroom that has no cleanroom. Every one names the outcome it serves and the evidence it produces for attainment.

    1. Simulate before you are taught

    Invert the usual order. Students run the process flow in SEMulator3D in the tutorial hour before the lecture that explains it, and write down one prediction and one thing that surprised them. The lecture then answers questions they already hold. This is predict–observe–explain applied to a process flow, and it converts the lecture from transmission into resolution. Serves CO3, CO5. Evidence: prediction sheets, collected and compared against post-lecture revisions.

    2. Process archaeology

    Give students a finished 3D model and no recipe. They must reconstruct the process sequence that produced it, from the geometry alone — spacer shapes reveal deposition and etch order, dishing reveals CMP, the absence of an interfacial layer reveals a clean. Reversing the flow tests structural understanding in a way that running it forward never does. Serves CO3, CO4. Evidence: reconstructed step lists marked against the true recipe.

    3. The split-cohort process window

    Each team is assigned one parameter — spacer thickness, overpolish, lateral ratio, anneal budget — and sweeps it alone. In the final session the class assembles every team's result into a single process window on one chart. No individual could have produced it; the cohort collectively discovers that windows are multidimensional and that margins interact. Serves CO4. Evidence: the assembled window, plus each team's contribution.

    4. Failure-mode laboratory

    One assignment in three is issued deliberately broken — a misplaced metrology mask, a missing etch stop, an anneal in the wrong position. The deliverable is the diagnosis, not the fixed model. Engineers in a fab spend far more time on why a build failed than on builds that work, and assessing debugging directly is both more authentic and far harder to plagiarise. Serves CO4, CO5. Evidence: diagnostic reports graded on Part D of the rubric.

    5. Layout in the morning, cross-section in the afternoon

    The hardest conceptual leap in Unit 1 is that a coloured polygon on a layout screen becomes a physical shape in silicon. Pair the two sessions on the same day and on the same design: students draw the inverter layout, then build it and slice it. The mapping stops being an assertion. Serves CO1. Evidence: annotated layout-to-cross-section overlays.

    6. Technology review panel

    Teams present a process module to the class as if to a fab technology review — fifteen minutes, questions from a student panel briefed to probe cost, thermal budget and yield. Rotating the panel role teaches students to interrogate a flow, which is the skill Unit 4 and Unit 5 actually assess. Serves CO4. Evidence: presentation and peer-question rubric.

    7. Cost per mask layer

    Unit 2 ends with a decision, not a summary: given a target pitch, argue for SADP or for EUV. Teams are given mask counts, tool costs and throughput, and must defend a recommendation. It puts the economic argument that drives real patterning choices in front of students who otherwise learn multiple patterning as a sequence of diagrams. Serves CO2, CO4. Evidence: written recommendation with quantified reasoning.

    8. Viva at the viewer

    Replace part of the end-semester laboratory examination with a five-minute oral held at the 3D viewer. The examiner points at a feature; the student explains what made it. It is fast, it is almost impossible to prepare by rote, and it discriminates between students who ran the recipe and students who understood it. Serves CO3, CO5. Evidence: oral rubric, Parts B and D.

    Accessibility and inclusion

    Every video carries a transcript and burned-in captions. Every interactive object is keyboard-operable and reports its state in text, so that it works with a screen reader and in a low-bandwidth session where the animation may not load. Colour is never the only channel: the material legend labels every stratum by name as well as by colour, which also matters for the roughly one male student in twelve with a colour-vision deficiency — a real constraint in a subject taught almost entirely through coloured cross-sections.
